Therapist Website Management: Who Should Run Your Practice Website?

The site was built once. The person who built it is gone. Here is what a mental health practice website actually needs every month, the four people who could do it, and how to take the keys back.

You hired a new clinician in March. She is still not on your website.

The insurance list shows a panel you left last year. The telehealth line in the footer does not say which states. The last blog post is from 2023. Somebody asked who has the login, and the answer was a shrug and the name of a web designer who stopped replying to email.

This is the most common state of a small mental health practice website. Not broken. Just abandoned. And an abandoned site does not hold its rankings. Google keeps checking whether a page is still accurate and still useful. Every month nothing changes, the site quietly loses ground to practices whose pages do.

Therapist website management is the unglamorous fix. Someone owns the site, every month, as a job. The question is who.

What a Practice Website Needs Every Month

Most owners picture website work as a redesign every five years. The real work is small and constant. Here is the strike list for a typical therapy, psychiatry, or group practice:

  • Clinician changes. New hires get a bio page with credentials, licensure states, and specialties. Departures come down, or get redirected, so patients are not booking with someone who left.
  • Insurance and fees. Panels change. Private-pay rates change. The page has to match what the front desk tells callers.
  • Availability. “Accepting new clients” and waitlist notices go stale fast. A wrong one costs you calls or wastes them.
  • Telehealth. Every new state of licensure is a page update, and a chance to show up in searches from that state.
  • Technical checks. Broken links, forms that stop sending, pages that drop out of Google’s index, a plugin update that breaks the mobile menu.
  • Google Business Profile. Hours, categories, services, photos, and posts, kept in step with the website.
  • New content. One or two pages or posts a month built around what patients actually search, so the site keeps earning new rankings instead of only defending old ones.
  • Social. A steady LinkedIn or Instagram presence that points back to the site.

None of that is hard. All of it is easy to forget. That is why it does not get done.

The Four People Who Could Run It

Every practice ends up with one of these. Each has a real tradeoff.

1. Someone on staff

The office manager, a clinician who is “good with computers,” or the owner at 10 p.m. It costs nothing extra on paper. In practice it is the first task dropped when prior authorizations pile up, and very few people on a clinical team know schema, indexing, or Search Console. It works for typo fixes. It rarely works for rankings.

2. An agency

Agencies bring a team and a process. For a large health system, that fits. For a five-clinician practice, it often means a ticket queue for a two-line bio change, a monthly report nobody reads, and an account manager who changes every few months. Some agencies are excellent. The structure just was not built for a practice that needs small changes, fast.

3. A freelancer you call when something breaks

Cheap and quick for one-off fixes. But nobody is watching the site between calls, so problems are found by patients, not by the person paid to fix them. And there is no plan for content or rankings at all.

4. One practitioner retained to run it

A single person who owns the website, the technical SEO, the Google Business Profile, the content, and the social posts as one job. You text them; it gets done; they verify it live. They watch Search Console so they see a problem before a patient does. This is the model Phoenix Method runs for mental health practices, and it is the one our mental health SEO page lays out in full.

The test is simple. If a clinician left today, how many days until her page came down and her patients’ searches pointed somewhere useful? If the honest answer is “whenever someone remembers,” nobody is running your website.

When the Agency Disappeared: Taking Back the Keys

This is the autopsy we open most often. The practice paid someone to build the site, the relationship faded, and now nobody inside the practice can log in to anything. Before anyone can manage the site, you need to own it. Recover these, in this order:

  1. The domain. Find out which registrar holds it and whose account it sits in. If it is registered to the old agency, get it transferred to an account the practice controls. Losing the domain is the one mistake you cannot undo.
  2. Hosting and the website platform. WordPress, Squarespace, Wix, or a custom build: get an owner-level login in the practice’s name, not a shared one.
  3. Google Business Profile. Make sure the practice is the primary owner and the old vendor is removed or downgraded to manager.
  4. Google Search Console and Analytics. These hold your search history. Add the practice as owner on both.
  5. Email and forms. Confirm where contact form submissions are going. A form that still delivers to a former vendor’s inbox fails silently, and nobody notices until a patient says they never heard back.

Keep every login in a password manager the practice controls. Whoever runs the site next gets their own access, never the owner’s password. That way the next transition takes an afternoon, not a crisis.

What Good Management Looks Like Month to Month

Good therapist website management is boring on purpose. A typical month looks like this:

  • Requests handled as they come in. New clinician, panel change, holiday hours. Done and checked on the live site, usually within days, not weeks.
  • A technical pass. Crawl errors, indexing, page speed, and schema reviewed and fixed in priority order. That is what our technical SEO service covers.
  • The Google profile kept in step. Hours, services, and a post or two so the profile looks alive. See GBP optimization.
  • New content. A service page, a clinician page, or a blog post aimed at a real search your patients make.
  • An accuracy sweep. Clinicians, insurance, availability, and telehealth states checked against reality once a month.
  • A short plain-language update. What changed, what moved in Search Console, what is next.

The payoff is not a dramatic spike. It is compounding: a site that is always current, always crawlable, and adding a little more authority every month. That is the kind of patient work that outranks agencies charging three times as much.

Where to Start This Week

You do not need to hire anyone to take the first step. Open your own website on your phone and check three things: is every current clinician listed, is the insurance list right, and does the site say where you offer telehealth? Then try to log in to your domain registrar and your Google Business Profile.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does therapist website management include?

It covers the ongoing work that keeps a practice website accurate and visible: clinician and insurance updates, availability and telehealth changes, technical fixes like broken links and indexing problems, Google Business Profile upkeep, new content aimed at real patient searches, and regular reporting. It is a monthly job, not a one-time project.

Can I manage my therapy practice website myself?

You can handle small edits yourself on most platforms. The parts that usually get missed are the technical checks, Search Console monitoring, Google Business Profile upkeep, and steady new content. If you do it yourself, put a recurring monthly block on your calendar and keep a checklist so updates do not depend on memory.

What should I do if my web designer or agency stopped responding?

Start by confirming who owns your domain, hosting, website platform, Google Business Profile, and Search Console, and get owner-level access in the practice’s name for each. Check where your contact forms are delivering. Once the practice controls every account, you can bring in someone new without starting over.
