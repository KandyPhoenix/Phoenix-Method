Therapist Website Management: Who Should Run Your Practice Website?

There are four ways to run a practice website. Each one breaks in a predictable place. Here is how to choose, what to ask before you hire anyone, and the checklist that gets your logins back first.

Quick test. Who can log in to your domain registrar right now?

Not “who set it up.” Who can log in, today, without emailing anyone. Then ask the same question about your website platform, your Google Business Profile, and Google Search Console.

If the answers are “I think the designer has that” or a long pause, the real question is not how to improve your site. It is who is in charge of it. Therapist website management is a job, and every practice already has someone doing it. Sometimes that someone is nobody.

This post compares the four people who can hold that job, the questions that separate a good fit from a bad one, and the order to take your accounts back before you hand the site to anyone new.

The Four Options for Running a Practice Website

Every small mental health practice ends up with one of these, on purpose or by default. None of them is wrong for everyone. Each has a place where it quietly fails.

1. Someone on staff

Works when: the changes are small, the person has protected time for it, and the platform is simple.

Breaks when: clinical and admin work gets busy, which is always. Website tasks are the first thing pushed to next week. Staff also rarely have the technical side: indexing problems, schema, page speed, Search Console. The site stays tidy but stops growing. And when that person leaves, the knowledge leaves with them.

2. An agency

Works when: you need a big project done, like a full rebuild, a brand refresh, or a multi-location launch, and you have the budget for a team.

Breaks when: the work after launch is small and frequent. A two-line change becomes a ticket, a queue, and a review cycle. Account managers rotate. Reporting gets long while the edits you actually asked for wait. Some agencies handle small clients beautifully. The model just was not designed around a practice that needs quick, steady changes.

3. A freelancer on call

Works when: something specific is broken and you need it fixed this week.

Breaks when: nothing is visibly broken. Nobody is watching between calls, so problems are discovered by patients instead of by the person you pay. There is usually no plan for content, Google Business Profile, or rankings, because nobody asked for one.

4. One practitioner retained to run it

Works when: you want the website, technical SEO, Google Business Profile, content, and social handled as one job by one person you can text.

Breaks when: that person has no system and no backup, or holds your accounts in their own name. The fix is the same as for every option: the practice owns everything, and the practitioner works inside it with their own access.

Pick by the work, not the title. A rebuild is a project. Keeping a site current and growing is a relationship. Most practices hire for the project and never staff the relationship, which is exactly how sites go dark two years after launch.

Five Questions to Ask Before You Hire Anyone

Whoever you choose, ask these before you sign. The answers tell you more than any portfolio.

  1. “Whose name will my accounts be in?” The only right answer is yours. They get their own login with the access they need.
  2. “How fast does a small change go live, and how do I ask for one?” You want a clear channel and a realistic turnaround, not “submit a ticket and we will scope it.”
  3. “What happens every month even if I ask for nothing?” Good management includes proactive work: technical checks, Search Console review, Google Business Profile upkeep, and new content.
  4. “What will you tell me each month?” A short, plain-language note on what changed and what moved beats a 30-page export.
  5. “If we part ways, what do I get?” The answer should be: everything. Logins, files, and notes, handed over without a fight.

Be wary of anyone who promises a specific ranking by a specific date. Google decides rankings. A good partner controls the quality and consistency of the work, and says so.

Take Back the Keys: The Handoff Checklist

This is the autopsy we open most often: the site was built by someone who is no longer around, and nobody inside the practice can get in. Before anyone can run your website well, the practice has to own it. Recover access in this order, most critical first:

  1. Domain registrar. Find which registrar holds your domain and whose account it sits in. If it belongs to a former vendor, move it to an account the practice controls. Losing the domain is the one mistake that is very hard to undo.
  2. DNS. Confirm where DNS is managed. It is sometimes at the registrar and sometimes elsewhere. DNS controls where your site and your email point.
  3. Hosting and the website platform. WordPress, Squarespace, Wix, or custom: get an owner or administrator login in the practice’s name. Make sure backups exist and you know where they live.
  4. Google Business Profile. The practice should be the primary owner. Remove old vendors or lower them to manager.
  5. Google Search Console and Analytics. Add the practice as owner on both. They hold your search history, and you cannot get it back if the property is lost.
  6. Forms and intake links. Submit a test through every form and confirm it reaches the right inbox. Check that booking and intake links still point to your current system.
  7. Everything else. Directory listings, social accounts, and any paid tools or plugins billed to someone else’s card.

Store every login in a password manager the practice controls. Each person who works on the site gets their own access, never the owner’s password. When someone leaves, you remove one user. No crisis, no ransom email.

What to Hand the Next Person

Once you own the accounts, write a one-page site brief. It makes any transition, to staff, a freelancer, an agency, or a practitioner, take hours instead of weeks:

  • Account list. Every login from the checklist, where it lives, and who has access.
  • Who approves what. Which changes the office manager can request and which need the owner.
  • Source of truth. Where the current clinician roster, services, and fees are kept, so the website is updated from one place.
  • Known problems. Anything you already know is broken or out of date.
  • Goals. The services or locations you most want to grow.

With that brief in hand, the person running your site can spend the first month fixing and building instead of hunting for passwords. Frequently Asked Questions

What does therapist website management include?

It is the ongoing work of keeping a practice website accurate, working, and growing: content and page updates, technical checks, Search Console monitoring, Google Business Profile upkeep, new content aimed at real patient searches, and a short monthly report. It is a recurring job, not a one-time project.

Is an agency or a freelancer better for a small therapy practice?

It depends on the work. An agency fits large projects like a rebuild. A freelancer fits one-off fixes. For steady monthly upkeep, content, and local SEO, many small practices are better served by one retained practitioner. Whichever you choose, keep every account in the practice’s name.

What should I do if my web designer or agency stopped responding?

Recover access in order: domain registrar, DNS, hosting and website platform, Google Business Profile, then Search Console and Analytics. Test every form. Move each account into the practice’s name and store logins in a password manager you control. Then you can bring in someone new without starting over.
