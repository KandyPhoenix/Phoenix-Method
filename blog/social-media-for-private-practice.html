Social Media for Private Practice: What Helps You Get Found, and What Is Noise

Posting will not push your website up Google on its own. It still earns its place, if it points at the right pages and holds the right privacy lines. Here is what to post, where, and what to stop doing.

Your last Instagram post went up in March.

Before that, a burst of three in a week, right after someone said you should “be more active.” Then caseloads filled, a clinician left, the insurance list changed, and the account went quiet. Somewhere in the back of your mind it sits on the list of things you are failing at.

Good news, though. Social media for private practice is not the lever most owners think it is. It will not rescue a website nobody updates. But done on purpose, a few times a month, it does real work. This is the autopsy: what it does not do, what it does, and the plan one busy practice can actually keep.

What Social Media Will Not Do for Your Rankings

Start with the myth, because it wastes the most time.

Likes and followers do not rank your website. Google’s John Mueller has said publicly, more than once since 2015, that Google does not use social signals like likes and shares to rank pages. A post with 200 likes does not move your anxiety therapy page up a single spot.

Links in posts are not ranking votes. The link in your bio and the links in most posts carry a nofollow tag. Since 2019 Google has treated that tag as a hint rather than a hard rule, but those links are not counted the way a link from another website can be. Their job is to send people to your site.

Posting does not fix a broken site. If your clinician page still lists someone who left last spring, your insurance page names a panel you dropped, and your telehealth details are one line in the footer, every post you write sends people to that. Social amplifies whatever the website already says. Right or wrong.

So if the choice this month is between ten Instagram posts and fixing your services pages, fix the pages. That is where patients decide, and where Google decides.

What It Actually Does for a Small Practice

Where social earns its time is trust and recall.

  • It shows the practice is still open. An account that stopped posting two years ago leaves people wondering whether the practice stopped too.
  • It puts your name in front of people. Someone who remembers the practice name can search it later and land on your own site and profile instead of a directory.
  • It reaches referral sources. Primary care offices, school counselors, case managers, and other clinicians refer to practices they know. A LinkedIn company page is one way to stay in front of them.
  • It gives your content a second audience. A new blog post can reach readers on social the week it goes live, while it is still working its way up in search.

None of that needs daily posting. It needs consistency, a link to the right page every time, and an account the practice actually owns.

Every post should point at a page that is correct. Before you post about a new clinician, a new insurance panel, or telehealth in a new state, update that page on your website first. Then the post sends people somewhere true.

Your Google Business Profile Comes First

If you only have time for one channel, make it your Google Business Profile.

It lives on Google Search and Google Maps, right where someone is choosing a therapist near them. Updates you publish there show on your profile next to your hours, reviews, and phone number. Google does not promise posts raise your ranking, but they put fresh, accurate information in front of people at the exact moment they decide whether to call.

A Posting Plan One Person Can Keep

The plan that works is the one that survives a full caseload. Pick two channels, not five.

LinkedIn: for referral sources and peers

Post from a practice company page, not only your personal profile. Share a new service, a new clinician, or a plain-language answer to a question referral partners ask you. Professional and short. Public LinkedIn posts can be indexed by Google, so a good one can also turn up when someone searches your practice name.

Instagram and Facebook: for the people you serve

One idea per post. What a first session looks like. How telehealth works at your practice. Which insurance plans you take, linked to the page that lists them. Calm, clear, and useful. Facebook can carry the same post the same day, with the full link to the page you are talking about.

The rhythm

  • Two to four posts a month beats twenty in January and none after.

  • Every post links to one page. The service page, the clinician page, the insurance page. Not just the homepage.

Privacy Lines Every Practice Post Has to Hold

Mental health social media has rules most marketing advice ignores. These are the ones we flag for every practice. They are a starting point for your decision, not legal advice, so run anything unclear past your own compliance resources.

  • No client stories, quotes, or photos. Not even with names removed. Small communities recognize details.
  • Never confirm someone is a client. Not in a comment reply, a review reply, or a direct message. A friendly “so glad our sessions helped!” confirms a treatment relationship in public. In 2023 a New Jersey psychiatric practice agreed to pay HHS $30,000 after its replies to negative online reviews disclosed patients’ health information.
  • Do not do therapy in the comments. Point anyone in crisis to emergency services or a crisis line, and move everything else to a private, secure channel.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does social media help a therapy practice rank on Google?

Not directly. Google has said it does not use likes or shares to rank pages, and links in most social posts are nofollow. Social helps in other ways: it shows the practice is active, builds searches for your practice name, reaches referral sources, and sends people to the pages that do the ranking work.

How often should a private practice post on social media?

Two to four posts a month on one or two channels is enough for most small practices. Consistency matters more than volume. Each post should link to a specific, accurate page on your website, such as a service, clinician, or insurance page.

Which social media platforms are best for therapists?

Start with your Google Business Profile, since it appears on Google Search and Maps. Then pick two: LinkedIn for referral sources and peers, and Instagram or Facebook for the people you serve. Keep every account in the practice’s name.
