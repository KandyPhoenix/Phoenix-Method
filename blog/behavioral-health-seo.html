Behavioral Health SEO for Group Practices and Clinics

A group practice has more ways to be found than any solo therapist in town. Most group sites bury them on one team page. Here is the autopsy, the page structure that fixes it, and what to change every time a clinician joins or leaves.

Eight clinicians. One “Our Team” page. Eight headshots, eight two-sentence bios, and a phone number at the bottom.

That is the group practice website I see most often. It is also why the practice ranks for its own name and almost nothing else.

A group should be the easiest site in mental health to rank. You have more specialties, more modalities, more insurance panels, and often more locations than any solo therapist in your market. Each of those is a search somebody types into Google. Behavioral health SEO for a group comes down to two jobs: get that breadth onto pages Google can find, then keep those pages true as people come and go.

Why a Group Practice Site Plays by Different Rules

A solo therapist’s site has one person to introduce. A group’s site has a roster, and the roster changes.

  • The searcher is matching. Someone looking at a group usually wants a specific fit: a clinician who sees teens, takes their plan, does EMDR, and has an opening. If your site can’t answer all four on one path, they go back to the results.
  • Referral sources check you too. Primary care offices, school counselors, and case managers look you up before they send anyone. A wall of headshots doesn’t tell them who treats what.
  • The facts expire. Clinicians join, leave, go part-time, close their caseload, add a telehealth state, drop a panel. A solo site changes a few times a year. A group site should change most months.

The Autopsy: How Group Sites Bury Their Own Clinicians

When I open a group practice site for the first time, the causes of death repeat.

  • The single team page. Everyone on one URL. Google can’t rank one page for “trauma therapist,” “couples counseling,” and “child psychologist” at once, so it ranks it for none of them.
  • Ghost clinicians. Someone left your practice a year ago. Their bio is still live, still indexed, still pulling searches for their name. Those callers reach your front desk and hear they’re gone.
  • The missing new hire. Your newest clinician has an open caseload and no page, because the page was “going to get done.”
  • A list instead of pages. Anxiety, trauma, ADHD, grief, couples, and eating disorders in one bulleted list, with nothing behind any of them. A bullet point is not a page.
  • Copy-and-paste locations. Two offices, two location pages, same paragraph with the city swapped. Google treats near-duplicates as one page and picks which to show. Usually the wrong one.
  • One insurance list for everybody. Panels are listed for the practice, but each clinician is credentialed differently. The patient picks a clinician, calls, and learns that person is out of network.

Your roster is your keyword list. Every specialty, modality, population, panel, and licensed state your clinicians bring is a search somebody is already typing. Most groups never give them a page.

The Battle Plan: Build the Grid

Three kinds of pages. Every one links to the other two.

1. A real page for every clinician

Not a pop-up. Not an anchor on the team page. Its own URL, something like /team/jane-doe-lcsw/. On it: license type, the states they’re licensed in, the concerns and populations they work with, the modalities they’re trained in, the insurance panels they’re on, in person or video or both, and whether they’re taking new clients right now. Add Person schema so Google can tie the clinician to the practice.

2. A page for every concern and modality you treat

Anxiety. Trauma. EMDR. DBT. Couples. Teens. Each gets a page that explains what treatment looks like at your practice and names the clinicians who provide it. This is where most of the ranking happens. It is also the work our content SEO service does: writing those pages from what your clinicians actually offer, not from a template.

3. A page for every location and every telehealth state

Each office gets its own address, hours, and clinicians. Each state you serve by video gets a page naming the clinicians licensed in it. Different facts make different pages. That is what keeps them from reading as duplicates.

Then wire it together. The EMDR page links to the three clinicians who do EMDR. Each of them links back to EMDR, to their office, and to their telehealth states. A patient can come in through any door and reach a bookable person in two clicks. Google follows the same links and learns who does what.

Underneath, add MedicalClinic schema with each location, and keep the practice name, address, and phone identical everywhere they appear.

Hires and Departures: The Upkeep That Decides Rankings

Building the grid is the easy part. Keeping it true is the job.

When a clinician leaves: don’t just delete the page. A deleted page turns into a 404, and the searches it earned go with it. Pull them off every concern, location, and telehealth page the same day, then redirect their URL to the specialty page that fits their work best. If they had their own Google Business Profile listing at your address, settle what happens to it before their last day.

When a clinician joins: their page goes live in week one, not quarter two. Add them to every concern, office, and state page that fits. Then run the new URL through URL Inspection in Search Console and request indexing.

When anything else changes: a dropped panel, a closed caseload, a new state license. Each one is a website edit, and in most groups nobody owns those edits. The office manager is buried in credentialing. That gap is what our post on therapist website management is about: who runs the site, and what they need from you to keep it current.

What I Check First in a Group Practice’s Search Console

Four reports, in this order. Together they show where the grid is leaking.

  1. Pages report, “Not found (404).” Old clinician URLs pile up here after someone deletes them. Each one used to earn searches and now earns nothing. If a departed clinician’s name still shows in your queries, that URL needs a redirect.
  2. Pages report, “Crawled - currently not indexed.” Thin bios and copy-paste location pages land here. Google read them and decided they weren’t worth keeping. The fix is more specific content on each one, not more pages.
  3. Performance, filtered to pages containing /team/. This shows which clinicians get found and for what. Usually one or two carry the whole practice and the rest show almost nothing.
  4. Queries with a concern plus a place. “Trauma therapist” plus your city. “Teen counseling” plus your suburb. If those land on your homepage instead of a concern page, the concern page is missing or too thin.

None of that needs a paid tool. It needs someone with access who looks every month. When the list turns up redirects to fix, schema to add, or pages Google won’t index, that is our technical SEO service.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is behavioral health SEO?

It is SEO for practices that provide mental and behavioral health care, such as group practices, counseling centers, psychiatry clinics, and telehealth practices. It covers the clinician, concern, location, and insurance pages people search for, the technical setup that gets them indexed, and the Google Business Profile work behind the map results.

Should every clinician in a group practice have their own page?

Yes. A page with license type, licensed states, specialties, insurance panels, and availability gives Google something specific to rank and gives patients a way to choose. A shared team page rarely ranks for anything but the practice name.

What should we do with a clinician’s page after they leave?

Do not delete it and leave a 404. Remove the clinician from every specialty, location, and telehealth page, then redirect their URL to the most relevant specialty page.
