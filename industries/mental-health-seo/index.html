Why Mental Health Is Different

Therapy Search Is Specific and Serious

Mental health content falls under Google's YMYL (Your Money or Your Life) category, which means E-E-A-T signals — Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness — carry more weight than on almost any other kind of page. Generic SEO playbooks routinely fail for therapy practices because they miss the clinical authority requirements. Worse, they can quietly drift into compliance-sensitive territory — tracking pixels on appointment forms, review widgets, analytics setups. We avoid that territory entirely.

On top of YMYL, the patient search patterns are unusually specific. Patients don't just search "therapist near me" — they search "IFS therapy for anxiety," "EMDR for PTSD [city]," "trauma therapist that takes [insurance]," "Aetna therapist in [neighborhood]," "psychiatrist accepting new patients telehealth." Each of those is a distinct query with distinct intent, and each deserves its own dedicated page. Agencies that collapse everything into a single "therapy services" page leave all that traffic on the table.

And there is a third layer that the SEO industry mostly ignores. Mental health practice websites go stale fast. A clinician joins, a clinician leaves, a panel gets added, a panel gets dropped, telehealth rules change by state. Google reads an outdated site as an untrustworthy site. For a YMYL category, that is fatal. Half of mental health SEO is not clever keyword work. It is keeping the site true.

What this page is really about: helping a mental health practice show up when the right patient is searching for the right kind of help — with content written carefully for YMYL, a website that stays accurate, and a single point of contact who handles all of it.

The Autopsy

Why Most Practice Websites Are Dead

We audit a lot of therapy and psychiatry sites. The same findings show up over and over, and almost none of them are exotic. They are the symptoms of a website nobody owns.

Nobody Is Running It

The site was built once — by a designer, a nephew, a Squarespace afternoon — and has not been touched since. No one on staff has a login they trust. The practice has grown and the website describes the practice from two years ago.

The Clinician Pages Are Wrong

A provider who left eighteen months ago is still listed. The new psychiatric nurse practitioner is not. The one page a patient most wants to read, "who will I actually see," is inaccurate or missing. Google notices the inconsistency with your Business Profile and Psychology Today.

The Insurance List Is Stale

"We accept Aetna, Cigna, and BCBS" — except you dropped Cigna last spring. Every wrong insurance line produces a wasted intake call, and the "therapist that takes [insurance]" searches you should own land on a page that is quietly lying.

There Is No Telehealth Page

You see half your caseload on video. Your website has one sentence about it in the footer. "Online therapy [state]" and "telehealth psychiatrist [state]" are some of the highest-intent searches in the category and you have no page that can rank for them.

One Page Does All the Work

Anxiety, depression, trauma, couples, ADHD, medication management — all on a single "Services" page with one paragraph each. Google cannot rank one page for twelve different patient intents. Neither can you.

Psychiatry

SEO for Psychiatrists and Psychiatric Practices

Psychiatry search is not therapy search. A patient looking for a psychiatrist is usually looking for one of three things: medication management, a diagnostic evaluation, or a prescriber who takes their insurance and has an opening this month. Those are commercial, urgent queries. "Psychiatrist near me accepting new patients," "ADHD evaluation adult [city]," "telehealth psychiatrist [state]," "psychiatric nurse practitioner [insurance]." The practice that answers them with a specific page wins the call. The practice with a generic "Our Services" page does not.

Psychiatric practices also carry a heavier E-E-A-T load than most therapy practices. Prescribing is high-stakes YMYL. Google wants to see board certification, licensure, the prescriber's actual name and credentials on the page, and clinically accurate descriptions of what a medication consultation involves. Anonymous "our providers" copy fails that test. Named, credentialed, specific copy passes it.

  • Service pages per intent — medication management, psychiatric evaluation, ADHD assessment, TMS or other specialty services each get a page, not a paragraph.
  • Prescriber pages that carry authority — name, credentials, board certification, conditions treated, telehealth availability, and whether they are accepting new patients.
  • Insurance and self-pay clarity — a page that says exactly which panels you are on and what a self-pay evaluation costs. It is the most-searched and most-neglected page in psychiatry.
  • Telehealth footprint by state — if your prescribers are licensed in three states, you can rank in three states. Most psychiatric sites rank in zero because the page does not exist.

Group Practices

Group Practice and Clinic SEO

A group practice has an SEO advantage a solo practice does not: breadth. Six clinicians means six sets of specialties, modalities, populations, and insurance panels, which means dozens of specific pages a patient could land on. Most groups throw that advantage away by listing everyone on one "Our Team" page with a headshot and two sentences.

The group practice playbook is architecture. Every clinician gets a real page with credentials, specialties, and the concerns they treat. Every modality and every concern the practice covers gets its own page. Those pages link to each other and to the clinician who provides them, so a search for "DBT therapist [city]" lands on the DBT page, which points to the two clinicians who run DBT, each of whom shows as accepting new patients. That is a structure Google can read and a patient can use.

It is also the structure that breaks fastest when nobody maintains it. A clinician leaves and her page keeps ranking, sending patients to a provider who is gone. A new hire's specialties never get added. This is why group practices are the clients who most need the website managed, not just optimized once.

Telehealth

Telehealth and Online Therapy SEO

Telehealth changed the geography of mental health search. A practice in one city can serve a whole state, and in some cases several states, but only if Google knows that. A single footer line saying "telehealth available" does not create a state-wide ranking footprint. A dedicated page for "online therapy in [state]" with the licensure, the technology, the insurance situation, and the clinicians who offer it does.

  • One telehealth page per licensed state — "online therapy [state]," "telehealth psychiatrist [state]," "virtual counseling [state]." Each is a separate search with separate volume.
  • Licensure stated plainly — which clinicians are licensed where. It is an E-E-A-T signal and a conversion signal at the same time.
  • Local SEO still matters — a telehealth-only practice still needs a Google Business Profile and still wins "near me" searches in its home market. Telehealth adds reach; it does not replace local.
  • Platform and privacy honesty — name the video platform, say what a first session looks like, and keep the page free of tracking that does not belong on healthcare forms.

Frequently Asked

Mental Health SEO Questions

How does SEO for therapists differ from regular SEO?

Therapy practice SEO sits at the intersection of YMYL healthcare content and hyper-local search. Patients search by modality (IFS, CBT, EMDR), by specific concern (anxiety, trauma, OCD), by insurance status, and by location — often all at once. Content must be clinically accurate, written carefully for YMYL, and targeted at the specific patient searching for a specific kind of help.

Who should manage a therapy practice's website?

Someone whose job it is. In most small practices that is no one, which is why the site goes stale and rankings decay. The options are a staff member with time and web skills (rare), an agency (slow and expensive for small changes), or a single practitioner retained to run the site, the Google Business Profile, and the content as one job. Phoenix Method is the third option.

How should patient testimonials be handled on my site?

Testimonial handling in healthcare is a regulatory matter, not an SEO one. On our side, we default to external review platforms (Google, Psychology Today, TherapyDen) and don't advise on on-site testimonial decisions.

Should I rank for my specific modality (IFS, CBT, EMDR, etc.)?

Yes — absolutely. Modality-specific searches are high-intent and convert. "IFS therapy [city]," "EMDR for trauma," "DBT for BPD" are queries where a dedicated page with clinically accurate content outranks generic "therapist near me" pages. Your specializations are your differentiation.

Do I need separate content for sliding-scale or private-pay?

Yes. "Sliding scale therapist [city]," "Medicaid therapist," "private pay therapy," and "therapist that takes [insurance]" are all distinct searches with distinct patients. A clear page on fees, insurance, and sliding scale captures all of them and eliminates friction for fit-matching patients.

Is psychiatry SEO different from therapy SEO?

Yes. Psychiatry queries are more commercial and more urgent (medication management, evaluations, "accepting new patients"), and the E-E-A-T bar is higher because prescribing is high-stakes YMYL. Prescriber pages with real credentials and service pages per intent matter more than blog volume. See our psychiatrist SEO guide.

How do I handle online reviews compliantly?

Google reviews, Psychology Today, TherapyDen, and similar third-party platforms are where mental-health review strategy should live. Patients consent to those platforms separately and the review itself is their public statement. On-site patient-written content crosses into regulatory territory outside our scope.

What about telehealth — does SEO work differently?

Slightly. Telehealth practices can rank in broader geographic areas (state-wide rather than city-specific), which changes the keyword strategy. State licensure shapes which geographies you can target. We'll map your telehealth footprint against searchable intent and build one page per licensed state.

Can you handle our social media too?

Yes, as part of the ongoing engagement. LinkedIn and Instagram posts are written from the practice's own pages and posts, scheduled, and reviewed by you before they go up. Social does not rank your website on its own, but it keeps the practice visible, builds brand searches, and gives your content a second audience.

How long until my phone starts ringing?

Therapy SEO timelines vary by market, competition, and starting state. Map pack and local levers tend to move first with aggressive GBP work; modality-specific organic rankings build over a longer horizon; authority for competitive YMYL queries (e.g., "anxiety therapy [major city]") is a long game. Therapy SEO compounds — practices that stick with a coherent E-E-A-T strategy consistently outcompete bigger group practices that never invested.
