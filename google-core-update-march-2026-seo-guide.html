Google's March 2026 Core Update: What Changed and What to Do

The March 2026 Core Update rolled out between March 10 and March 27 — and it hit hard. Over 55% of monitored websites saw ranking shifts within the first two weeks. If you're in healthcare, dental, mental health, or any YMYL niche, this update demands your attention.

SEMrush Sensor volatility scores spiked to 9.5 out of 10. Some businesses reported organic traffic drops of 20-35% overnight. Here's what actually changed, who got hit, who gained, and exactly what you should be doing right now — without panic.

What Made This Update Different

This wasn't a routine tweak. Two things made March 2026 unusual.

First, it landed just weeks after Google's first-ever Discover-only core update finished on February 27. Back-to-back algorithm changes within a month is rare, even by Google's standards. Many site owners got whiplash — their Discover traffic shifted in February, then their organic rankings reshuffled in March.

Second, industry analysts believe this is the first core update leveraging Google's Gemini 4.0 Semantic Filter, which means Google is now using its most advanced AI to evaluate content quality at a level we haven't seen before.

The March update rolled out over a 19-day window, with a notable second wave of ranking changes around day eight that caught many off guard.

The Five Biggest Changes

1. Information Gain Is Now a Major Ranking Factor

Google is now measuring how much genuinely new information a page contributes compared to content that already ranks. This is a seismic shift.

Pages that reword existing top results without adding original data, case studies, or unique perspectives are losing ground fast. Sites that invest in original research and proprietary insights saw an average visibility gain of 22% during this update.

What this means for you: If your content can be found word-for-word on ten other websites, it's now a liability. Every page on your site needs to answer the question: what does this page offer that nobody else's does?

2. E-E-A-T Requirements Tightened — Especially for Healthcare

Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness requirements got stricter, particularly for YMYL topics like health, finance, and legal content.

The data is stark: 72-73% of top-ranking pages now display detailed author credentials, up from about 58% before the update. Google is no longer giving the benefit of the doubt to anonymous or poorly attributed content in sensitive niches.

For healthcare and therapy practices, this means your therapist bios, clinical credentials, license numbers, and professional affiliations are now directly tied to your search visibility. A page about "anxiety therapy" written by "Staff" is losing to a page written by a named, licensed clinician with verifiable credentials.

What this means for you: Every piece of YMYL content on your site needs a named author with visible credentials. If you run a dental practice, your blog posts should be attributed to your licensed dentists. If you run a therapy practice, your service pages should reference your therapists' specific training and license numbers.

3. AI-Generated Content Is Under Real Scrutiny

The March update doesn't penalize AI-assisted content outright — Google confirmed at the 2025 Search Central Meetup that AI content "isn't inherently problematic." The violation happens when content is published primarily to generate SEO traffic at scale without meaningful human oversight.

The targeting is specific: pages that read fluently but add nothing that can't be found elsewhere. Content that combines AI efficiency with genuine human expertise remains unaffected. Mass-produced AI content without editorial oversight is now a ranking liability.

What this means for you: Using AI tools to help draft content is fine. Publishing 50 AI-generated blog posts a month without having a human expert review, edit, and add original perspective to each one is not. Quality over quantity has never been more literal.

4. Core Web Vitals Are Being Evaluated Site-Wide

Technical performance crossed a new threshold in this update. Google appears to be evaluating Core Web Vitals more holistically across your entire site rather than page by page. A fast homepage doesn't save you if your blog posts are slow.

The three metrics that matter:

  • Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): How fast your largest on-page element loads. Target: under 2.5 seconds.
  • Interaction to Next Paint (INP): How quickly your site responds to every user interaction. 43% of sites still fail the 200ms threshold.
  • Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): How much your page elements jump around while loading.

Data indicates 47% of sites with performance issues experienced ranking drops during this update. Google isn't punishing slow sites directly — it's promoting faster competitors above you.

What this means for you: Run every key page through PageSpeed Insights, not just your homepage. Image optimization, lazy loading, and minimizing third-party scripts are your quickest wins.

5. Local and Discover Signals Were Recalibrated

The February Discover update tightened which sites appear in Google Discover feeds — the number of unique domains in US Discover feeds dropped from 172 to 158. Google is showing users more locally relevant content, reducing clickbait, and favoring original reporting.

The March core update compounded this by adjusting how local relevance factors into organic rankings. Proximity data and behavioral signals now influence rankings more than before, particularly for service-based businesses.

What this means for you: Your Google Business Profile, local citations, NAP consistency, and geo-targeted content matter more than ever. Make sure your content reflects your geography naturally — not just in a footer address, but in your service pages, blog posts, and meta data.

Who Won and Who Lost

Winners

  • Sites with original research and expert commentary
  • Industry publications with first-hand experience
  • Healthcare sites with clear author credentials
  • Strong local SEO foundations and active GBP
  • Sites investing in Core Web Vitals

Losers

  • Mass-produced AI content without oversight
  • Thin, dynamically generated pages
  • Content aggregators rewording existing results
  • YMYL sites without clear authorship
  • Inconsistent technical performance

What to Do Right Now: Your 6-Step Plan

Don't panic. Don't start rewriting everything on day one. The March 2026 rollout officially finished on March 27 — so now is the time to act with precision, not panic.

Step 1: Measure the Damage (or the Gains)

Open GA4 and compare your organic traffic for the two weeks before March 10 against the two weeks after. Look at both sessions and engaged sessions. Open Google Search Console and check impression and click trends at both the query and page level. Check Discover traffic separately.

Step 2: Audit Your E-E-A-T Signals

Review every service page and blog post. Does it have a named author with visible credentials? Can a visitor verify that expertise? Does it demonstrate first-hand experience? For healthcare sites: add license numbers, link to professional profiles, mention specific training.

Step 3: Eliminate Thin and Duplicate Content

Identify pages that don't add unique value. If a blog post says the same thing as fifty other sites, either add original perspective or remove it. Quality dispersion is real — strong content loses weight when surrounded by weak pages on the same domain.

Step 4: Fix Technical Performance Across Your Entire Site

Run PageSpeed Insights on your homepage, your top 5 service pages, and your most-visited blog posts. Compress images to WebP, enable lazy loading, minimize third-party scripts, fix layout shifts.

Step 5: Strengthen Your Local SEO Foundation

Update your Google Business Profile completely. Audit NAP consistency across all citations. Create or update geo-targeted content for the areas you serve. Post weekly and respond to every review.

Step 6: Add Original Value to Existing Content

Don't just refresh dates and republish. Google confirmed that updating dates without updating substance is a weak move. For every key page, ask: after reading this, does the visitor know something they couldn't learn anywhere else?

The Bottom Line

The March 2026 Core Update is not a punishment — it's a recalibration. Google is getting better at distinguishing between content that genuinely helps people and content that exists primarily to capture search traffic. The businesses that treat their website as a resource for their customers rather than a keyword-targeting machine will continue to rise.

If you saw drops, there's a path back. It starts with honest assessment, not panic.
